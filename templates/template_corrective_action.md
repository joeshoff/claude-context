```markdown
---
type: corrective_action
register: direct, clinical
audience: direct report (individual); occasionally CC'd HR or skip-level
when_to_use: >
  A performance or behavior gap has been identified, documented, and the
  direct report needs a clear statement of what is broken, what the evidence
  is, and what changes immediately. Use when coaching has already occurred and
  this is the formal or near-formal record. Not for first-pass feedback in a
  1:1.
---

## Structural Pattern

```
1. DIAGNOSIS — name the gap in the first sentence. No warm-up.
2. EVIDENCE — embed the specific instances. Not a numbered list of sins;
   woven into the statement so the gap and the proof arrive together.
3. PRIOR COACHING — establish that this is not news to the recipient.
   "I asked for..." / "We discussed..." / "You were told..."
4. EXPECTATION — state what changes, by when, and under what condition.
   Decision-rule form: "If [condition], then [consequence]."
5. PATH — one sentence on what success looks like from here.
   Optional: close with the door open to a specific, bounded conversation.
   No open-ended invitations.
```

---

## Template

**Subject:** [Performance concern / Role expectation / Specific gap topic] — [Name, optional]

[Name],

[One sentence: name the gap directly. No "I wanted to reach out" or context-setting. Example: "The pattern I'm seeing is [X]."]

[Evidence block — 2–4 sentences. Name specific dates, outputs, or behaviors. Embed the coaching history here: "I asked for [X] on [date/occasion]. That didn't happen." If there is documentation, reference it by name rather than quoting it in full. Do not soften with "I understand there were challenges."]

[Prior coaching — 1–2 sentences. "We discussed this on [date/in [forum]]." If there is a prior corrective action, name it. "This is the second time I've named this directly."]

[Expectation — decision-rule form. "Effective immediately, I expect [specific behavior/output]. If [condition not met] by [date/next check-in/next sprint], [named consequence]." Be literal. "Improvement" is not a target; a specific deliverable or observable behavior is.]

[Path forward — one sentence. What does compliance look like? What does success allow? "If [X is true], we can [move forward / close this out / revisit the conversation]."]

[Optional: If there is a genuine process question or dispute about the facts, name it explicitly and close it. "If you believe the record is wrong on [specific point], bring that to me by [date]."]

[Sign-off — no "let me know your thoughts." Close flat or with a specific next step.]

Joe

## Annotated Examples

### Example A — Email to HR, defending manager's documentation ahead of termination

> **What's happening:** Joe's manager (Rebecca) has made a termination decision. HR (Bridget)
> is pumping the brakes on documentation sufficiency. Joe is correcting that read and
> asserting the decision will move forward.

**Diagnosis first:**
> "To my mind, there is ample evidence of coaching from Rebecca and repeated failures to
> comply with that coaching..."

Joe doesn't open with "I understand your concern." He opens with his conclusion. The
evidence ("what she has provided below...previous corrective actions") is embedded in the
sentence that delivers the verdict, not listed separately. Note the phrase "unfortunately,
have not resulted in a maintained consistency of results" — the word "unfortunately" is the
*only* empathy token in the message. One word. That's the budget.

**Decision rule, not hedge:**
> "I expect we will be starting termination proceedings in Workday by the end of the day."

Not "we are planning to" or "we may." "I expect." The only conditional offered is an
explicit escalation path ("I am happy to take this up with Hank and SaraR") — and even
that is framed as a challenge to Bridget's position, not a genuine invitation to reverse
course.

**Voice note:** When the corrective action is about defending a decision already made,
the template collapses the evidence and conclusion into one move. You are not building
a case; you are stating a judgment.

---

### Example B — Teams chat, correcting a team lead on work prioritization

> **What's happening:** Blake's team is spreading work across too many parallel tracks.
> Joe has interrogated Blake directly and extracted a commitment. He documents the
> diagnosis, the evidence, and the consequence in a chat thread.

**Diagnosis via interrogation:**
> "Blake, upon my interrogation, agreed that they should 'stack' the work..."

Joe uses the word "interrogation" without apology. The gap (parallel work instead of
sequenced) is named immediately. Blake's own admission is the first piece of evidence —
Joe doesn't have to argue; the subject agreed.

**Evidence embedded, not appended:**
> "the time allocation report shows... the allocation report... according to JIRA."

Three separate data sources named in the same breath as the diagnosis. No "here are the
details:" preamble.

**Expectation as decision rule:**
> "I was very clear that this gap needs to close by at least 3 weeks by next sprint
> otherwise, they are just treading water."

The consequence ("treading water") is concrete. The timeline ("next sprint") is named.
The metric ("close by at least 3 weeks") is specific. This is not vague improvement
language.

**Voice note:** In Teams/chat, Joe compresses. The corrective content arrives inside
operational context — he doesn't break out into a formal tone. The register is direct
but reads as in-the-work, not ceremonial.

## Voice Notes: What Makes This Type Distinctive in Joe's Register

**1. The diagnosis is the opening move, not the warm-up.**
Joe never front-loads empathy or context. The gap is named in sentence one. Everything
after is evidence and expectation. If there is a relationship acknowledgment, it is one
word ("unfortunately") or one clause, not a paragraph.

**2. Evidence is woven, not listed.**
A bulleted list of failures reads like a dossier. Joe embeds evidence into the diagnostic
sentence. This communicates that the evidence is obvious, not that it needs to be
cataloged. It also prevents the recipient from treating the list as negotiating points.

**3. Prior coaching is stated as fact, not reminded.**
"I asked for..." "We discussed..." "This is the second time." No softening. No "as you
may recall." The coaching history is treated as shared, documented reality.

**4. Expectations are decision rules, not aspirations.**
"If [X] by [date], then [consequence]" — not "I'd like to see improvement." The
consequence named should be one the writer will actually execute. If you write it, mean it.

**5. The only door left open is specific and bounded.**
Joe sometimes leaves a challenge path: "If you believe the record is wrong on X, bring it
to me by [date]." This is not an open invitation to relitigate everything. It is a named,
time-boxed objection window. After that window, the decision stands.

**6. No sword-falling, no excessive empathy performance.**
Joe does not write "I know this is difficult" or "I want to support your success." The
corrective action *is* the support. Naming the gap clearly and setting an explicit
expectation is treated as the professional courtesy, not the condemnation.
```
